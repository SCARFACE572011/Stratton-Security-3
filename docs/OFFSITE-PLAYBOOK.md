# Stratton Security Group — Off-Site Growth Playbook

This document covers everything that has to happen **outside** the website to make Stratton Security Group findable — in Google's map results, and in the answers people now get from ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, and Google's own AI summaries. Almost none of it is developer work. It is claiming profiles, asking clients for reviews, getting real photographs taken, and keeping the company's name, address and phone number identical everywhere. The office manager can execute most of it; Omar and the account managers own the parts that require talking to clients. Right now this work matters more than adding more pages to strattonsecuritygroup.com, for one blunt reason: the website is already strong — ten service pages, twenty city pages, guides, schema, fast and indexable — and it is being held back by the fact that Stratton has **no Google Business Profile and zero Google reviews**. A better website cannot fix that. Only the items below can.

Everything here is written to be executed by a non-technical person. Where a step needs the developer, it says so explicitly and tells you exactly what to send them.

## Start Here: the four things that matter most

Ranked. If you only ever do the first two, you will still have captured most of the available upside.

### 1. Claim and verify the Google Business Profile
**Who:** office manager, with Omar available for the verification video.
**Effort:** about one working day of actual effort, spread over one to three weeks (verification is the slow part and is out of your control).
**Impact:** the highest of anything in this document. Without a verified profile, Stratton **cannot appear at all** in the map box that sits above the normal results when someone searches "security guard company near me." That box takes most of the clicks on a phone. It also feeds nearly every other source in this playbook: "best of" listicles, AI answers, and Apple/Bing listings all read from it.
**Honest caveat:** claiming the profile does not produce rankings by itself. It makes Stratton *eligible*. Item 2 is what moves you up.

### 2. Start the Google review engine
**Who:** the account manager or supervisor the client already knows makes the ask; the office manager runs the tracking sheet and follow-ups.
**Effort:** about 30 minutes a week, forever. It never becomes "done."
**Impact:** review count and rating are the biggest ranking inputs you actually control, and reviews are the single most-quoted source when an AI model decides which local company to name. Stratton has zero. Comparable LA firms have 20–150.
**Honest caveat:** this is slow and compounding — 6 to 18 months, not weeks. Anyone promising faster is selling fake reviews, which will cost you the profile.

### 3. Get real photography taken
**Who:** the client. This is a purchase and a scheduling decision — nobody else can do it.
**Effort:** half a day of shooting, $800–2,000 for a competent LA commercial photographer (or a genuinely capable person with a modern phone and good light).
**Impact:** stock photography is **banned** on Google Business Profiles and can get photos removed or the profile suspended. It also fails the human test — a property manager comparing three vendors spots a stock guard photo instantly. The same shoot also replaces the stock imagery currently on the website. One shoot, two problems solved.
**Honest caveat:** this is the step that quietly never happens. Put a date on the calendar this week.

### 4. Fix the entity trail — the five URLs and the Bing/Apple/LinkedIn listings
**Who:** office manager to create the listings; then one email to the developer with five URLs.
**Effort:** roughly a day of clicking, spread over a few weeks of waiting for verifications. The developer's part is five minutes.
**Impact:** the website already has five empty slots waiting for Stratton's LinkedIn, Yelp, Google, BBB and Bark URLs. Filling them tells search engines and AI models "the company on this website and these other profiles are all one business." Bing matters more than its market share suggests, because **ChatGPT's web search retrieves from Bing's index**.
**Honest caveat:** citations don't make you rank. They are the price of admission — what makes you eligible to appear and confident enough for a model to name you.

# Section 1 — Google Business Profile

This is the single highest-leverage item in the entire plan. Stratton has a real office, a real license, real marquee clients, and zero presence on the one surface that decides who gets called when someone in Century City searches "security guard company near me" on their phone. Everything else in this document is worth less until this is done.

**Honest expectation-setting before you start:** claiming and filling out the profile takes about one working day of effort spread over one to three weeks. It does not produce rankings by itself. A complete, verified, photo-rich profile makes you *eligible* to appear in the Google map pack; reviews and consistency over the following months are what actually move you up it. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

### 1.1 Before you create anything: check whether a profile already exists

Businesses often already have an unclaimed profile that Google generated automatically, or one an old marketing vendor made. Creating a second one is a duplicate, and duplicates get one or both listings suspended.

1. Open Google Maps in a browser (not the app) and search each of these, one at a time:
   - `Stratton Security Group`
   - `Stratton Security`
   - `10940 Wilshire Blvd Suite 1720`
   - `Stratton Security Group Los Angeles`
2. If a listing appears with a **"Claim this business"** or **"Own this business?"** link — **claim that one.** Do not start fresh. It may already carry a few reviews, photos, or a history that a brand-new listing would not.
3. If a listing appears and says **"Verified"** but you did not verify it, someone else controls it. Click through to **"Request access."** The current owner has three days to respond; if they do not, you can usually claim it. If a former web vendor or ex-employee owns it, email them directly and ask them to transfer ownership — that is faster than Google's process.
4. If nothing exists at all, go to **google.com/business** and choose **Add your business**.

Do all of this from a Google account you will still control in five years. Use a company account (e.g. the Info@ or omar@ address on Google Workspace), **not** a personal Gmail belonging to one employee. Then add a second person as a **Manager** on day one so no single person is a bottleneck.

### 1.3 Verification: what to expect for a suite in an office tower

Google will pick the verification method for you. You do not get to choose, though you can sometimes request an alternative if the first one fails. For a Suite address in a multi-tenant Class A building in Westwood, expect **video verification** most of the time.

#### If you get video verification (most likely)

You record a single, unbroken video on your phone — you cannot stop, cut, or edit it — proving three things: that the location is real, that the business operates there, and that you are authorized to manage it. Do not start recording until you can show all of the following in one continuous take:

1. **Street-level exterior:** the building with the visible street number `10940` and the Wilshire Blvd street sign in the same shot. Walk a few steps so it is obviously live footage.
2. **Walk into the building:** lobby, then the building directory or elevator bank. If Stratton is listed on the lobby directory board, get a clear shot of it — this is strong evidence.
3. **Up to the floor and to the suite door:** show the `1720` suite number, and — this is the part that fails people — **Stratton signage on or beside the door.**
4. **Inside the office:** desks in use, computers on, branded material (a Stratton uniform, a business card, letterhead, the framed license, vehicle keys, a marked jacket).
5. **Proof you run it:** hold up something tying you to the business — a business card with your name, the CA PPO #122163 license certificate, a utility or lease document with the business name, or the office keys while unlocking the door.

**Get signage on the suite door before you record.** A printed vinyl or acrylic plaque reading "Stratton Security Group" is a $60–150 item and its absence is the most common reason a video verification is rejected. If building management controls the door plaques, request one now — it can take a week or two.

Record during business hours with people present. An empty, dark office at 9pm reads as fake.

#### If you get postcard verification

Google mails a five-digit code to `10940 Wilshire Blvd Suite 1720`. It typically arrives in **5 to 14 days**, sometimes longer.

- Tell the building's mailroom and front desk to expect mail for "Stratton Security Group" and to hold it. Postcards addressed to a business name the mailroom doesn't recognize get returned to sender, and that is the single most common failure.
- **Do not edit the profile while waiting.** Changing the name, address, category, or hours mid-verification cancels the postcard and restarts the clock.
- If it hasn't arrived after 14 days, request a second one from the profile dashboard. If a second fails, use the **"I did not receive my code"** or support-contact link and ask for an alternative method, providing the PPO license and lease as evidence.

#### If you get phone, email, or instant verification

Fastest case. Answer the call or check the inbox and enter the code. Instant verification sometimes happens automatically if the same Google account already verified `strattonsecuritygroup.com` in Google Search Console — which it has. That may work in your favor.

#### If verification is denied

It happens, especially for suites and for security companies. Do not create a second profile — that makes it worse. Use the appeal / reinstatement form, and attach:

- The CA BSIS PPO License #122163 certificate showing the business name
- The signed lease or a rent invoice for Suite 1720
- A photo of the door signage and lobby directory
- A utility bill or business bank statement at that address

Appeals typically take one to three weeks. Be patient and do not resubmit repeatedly.

### 1.4 Categories — the biggest single ranking lever on the profile

Your **primary category** determines which searches you are even considered for. It matters more than anything else in this section.

**Primary category: `Security guard service`**

Type it into the category box exactly and pick the exact match. Do not pick "Security system supplier," "Security system installer," or "Alarm company" — those are for people who sell and install hardware, and choosing one would put Stratton in the wrong competitive set entirely.

**Secondary categories** (Google allows up to 9; add only 2–4):

| Candidate | Add it? |
|---|---|
| Security service | Yes — broad match, safe |
| Bodyguard service | Yes — executive protection is a genuine, live Stratton service line |
| Fire protection service | Only if the exact name appears when you type it, and only because fire watch is a real Stratton service. If it looks like it's meant for sprinkler-system contractors, skip it |
| Event management company | No — Stratton provides event *security*, not event production |
| Patrol service / Security patrol | Add if it exists as an exact option in the dropdown |

**The rule:** if the category name does not appear in the dropdown as you type, it does not exist. Never pick a "close enough" category. Every secondary category slightly dilutes the primary signal, so fewer and more accurate beats more and looser.

Once set, leave the primary category alone. Changing it later resets some of Google's confidence in the listing.

### 1.5 Display the address, or hide it?

Google gives you two modes, and the choice has real consequences.

| | **Display the address** | **Hide it (service-area business)** |
|---|---|---|
| Map pin & "Directions" button | Yes | No |
| Eligible for the local map pack | Yes | Yes |
| Google's requirement | Must have permanent signage, be staffed during your stated hours, and be able to meet customers there | You must serve customers at *their* location, not yours |
| Buyer perception | Stronger — a property manager evaluating a security vendor wants to see a real Westwood office | Slightly weaker; some buyers read a hidden address as "guy with a truck" |
| Risk | If you can't meet the criteria and a competitor reports you, you can be suspended | Low |

**Recommendation: display the address**, provided all three of these are true — and they should be, because it's a leased Class A suite:

1. There is (or will be) permanent Stratton signage on the suite door or the lobby directory.
2. Someone is physically in Suite 1720 during normal business hours.
3. A prospective client could book a meeting there and be received.

If any of those is not true today, fix it before verifying rather than hiding the address. The Westwood office is a genuine credibility asset. If it genuinely cannot be met, set Stratton up as a service-area business instead: enter the address for verification, then tick "I deliver goods and services to my customers" and hide the address from public view. That is fully policy-compliant.

**Kill the biggest myth right now:** hiding your address does *not* spread you across Los Angeles. Google still uses the hidden address as the center point for distance calculations. A service-area business with a Westwood address ranks in Westwood-proximate searches exactly like a displayed one. Choosing "service area business" is a compliance decision, not a ranking strategy.

Never list a client's property, a mailbox store, a coworking desk, or a virtual office as the address. That is the fastest route to a permanent ban, and it is exactly the pattern Google's spam team hunts for in the security industry.

### 1.8 Hours: open 24 hours, seven days

Set **Open 24 hours** for Monday through Sunday. The website says 24/7/365; the profile must agree.

**One honest condition:** Google's guideline is that hours reflect when you are reachable and open for business. Setting "Open 24 hours" means someone answers (424) 440-5554 at 3am on a Sunday. Stratton runs a live 24/7 operations center, so this is defensible and true. But if there is any hour of the week where that number rings out to voicemail, either fix that or set real hours instead. A "Call now" button at 2am that nobody answers turns a lead into a bad review.

If you want to be precise about the difference between "the phone is answered around the clock" and "you can walk into Suite 1720 at midnight," say so in the business description or in a Google Post — not by shortening the hours.

Also do this:

- **Holiday hours:** Google will prompt you before Thanksgiving, Christmas, New Year's, July 4th, etc. Confirm "Open 24 hours" each time. If you ignore the prompt, Google displays "Hours might differ" next to your listing during exactly the weeks when event and retail security demand peaks.
- Do not use the "More hours" section unless you have a genuinely different schedule for something specific.

### 1.10 Photos — the part most companies do badly

Profiles with real, plentiful photos get materially more clicks and calls than bare ones. This is also where Stratton has a genuine advantage over the sea of generic guard companies: you have uniformed officers, marked vehicles, a Class A Westwood office, and a real brand.

#### Stock photography is banned — no exceptions

Google's photo guidelines require images to be **authentic representations of your business**, taken by you or on your behalf. Uploading stock images (Unsplash, Shutterstock, Getty, or anything pulled off the web) is a policy violation. Consequences range from silent removal of the photos, to loss of the ability to upload, to profile suspension if it's egregious. It also fails the human test: a property manager comparing three vendors can spot a stock photo of a generic guard instantly, and it reads as "this company has nothing real to show."

**This means a photo shoot is required, and it is a client deliverable.** The website is currently running on stock imagery too, and the same shoot solves both problems. Budget half a day and either a local commercial photographer ($800–2,000 in LA) or a genuinely competent person with a modern phone shooting in good light. Do not upload the website's current stock images to the profile.

#### Shot list — aim for 20–30 images at launch

| Category | What to shoot | How many |
|---|---|---|
| **Logo** | The Stratton logo, square, clean background, 720×720 or larger | 1 |
| **Cover** | The strongest single image — a uniformed officer at a recognizable LA property, or the marked patrol vehicle. Wide/landscape | 1 |
| **Exterior** | 10940 Wilshire Blvd from the street, the building entrance, the lobby, the Suite 1720 door with signage | 4–5 |
| **Interior** | The office in use — desks, monitors, dispatch/operations area, the framed PPO license, a meeting room | 4–5 |
| **Team** | Officers in uniform: full length, on post, at a lobby desk, in a vehicle, on foot patrol, an armed officer and an unarmed officer | 6–8 |
| **Vehicles** | Marked patrol vehicle exterior, the logo/livery detail, a vehicle at night with lighting | 3–4 |
| **At work** | Access control at a gate, radio/reporting device in use, a shift briefing, uniform and equipment detail | 4–5 |

#### Technical requirements

- JPG or PNG, at least 720 × 720 pixels, between 10 KB and 5 MB
- Well-lit, in focus, level. Shoot in the late afternoon for exteriors
- **No watermarks, no logo overlays, no text banners, no heavy filters.** Google strips or rejects these
- Shoot both daytime and nighttime — nighttime patrol images are distinctive and most competitors don't have them

#### Two consent issues you must handle — these are not optional

1. **Officers must consent to being photographed and published.** Get a short written sign-off from each person who appears. Some officers, particularly those with law-enforcement backgrounds or executive protection assignments, will have real objections. Respect them and shoot someone else.
2. **Do not photograph a client's property without written permission from that client**, and do not publish anything that reveals a client's security posture — camera positions, guard schedules, access-control points, gate codes, patrol routes, alarm panels. For a security company this is a genuine liability, not a legal technicality. If in doubt, shoot at the Wilshire office, in a parking structure, or at a location Stratton controls.

#### Ongoing

Add 3–5 new photos per month, permanently. It's a small, steady freshness signal and it keeps the profile from looking abandoned. Assign this to the office manager as a recurring calendar task. The same photo library feeds every other listing in Section 3 and every Google Post.

### 1.11 What NOT to do — the suspension list

Read this section twice. Recovering a suspended profile takes weeks, reviews are sometimes lost permanently, and the disruption to inbound leads is severe.

| Don't | Why |
|---|---|
| **Add keywords to the business name** — "Stratton Security Group Security Guards Los Angeles," "Stratton Security Group \| Armed Guard Services" | The #1 suspension cause in this industry. The name must match real-world signage, the license, and legal registration. Competitors actively report it. The short-term ranking bump is real; so is losing the profile |
| **Create extra listings for each city** — a "Stratton Security Group – Pasadena" profile at a virtual office or a client's site | Every one of those is a fake location. Google's detection is good and getting better, and a fake-location finding can take down your *real* profile too |
| **Use a UPS Store box, virtual office, coworking desk, or registered-agent address** | Prohibited. Only real, staffed locations qualify |
| **Ask only happy clients for reviews ("review gating")** | Explicitly against Google policy, whether done by hand or by software. Ask everyone or ask no one |
| **Offer anything for a review** — discounts, gift cards, a raffle entry, even a coffee | Prohibited by Google, and illegal under the FTC's fake-review rule |
| **Write reviews yourself, ask staff or family to, or buy them** | Detectable, removable, and grounds for suspension. Employee reviews from the office Wi-Fi are a well-known pattern Google filters |
| **Set hours you don't actually keep** | Undermines the "Call now" experience and invites bad reviews |
| **Upload stock photos** | See 1.10 |
| **Repeat cities in the description** to hit keywords | Gets the description rejected; at scale, gets the profile flagged |
| **Let an outside "GBP optimization" vendor change the name or categories** | Most of them do exactly the things in this table. If you hire help, put "do not modify the business name" in writing |
| **Create a duplicate profile when verification stalls** | Turns a delay into a suspension. Appeal instead |
| **Edit the profile while a verification postcard is in the mail** | Cancels verification and restarts the clock |

### 1.12 Q&A — seed it yourself, from the site FAQ

The Q&A section on your profile is public and **anyone can post a question, and anyone can answer it** — including a competitor, or a member of the public who guesses wrong. Leaving it blank is how you end up with a bad answer sitting on your listing.

Google explicitly permits business owners to post their own frequently asked questions and answer them. This is not review gating and it is not against policy — it's the intended use of the feature.

**How to do it (the mechanics matter):**

1. Have the owner or office manager post the **question** while signed into their **personal** Google account (Q&A can't be posted as the business).
2. Then answer it while signed into the **business** account, so the reply carries the "Owner" badge — which is what makes it trustworthy to readers.
3. Upvote each answer from a couple of accounts. Answers with more upvotes surface first.
4. Keep answers short — roughly 300 characters or fewer. Google truncates long ones and readers skim.

**Seed these eight, in this order** (all drawn from the real site FAQ, so they're already consistent with the rest of your content):

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Is Stratton Security Group licensed and insured? | Yes. Stratton holds California PPO License #122163 from the Bureau of Security and Investigative Services. All officers are individually licensed, and the company is fully bonded and insured. |
| How quickly can Stratton deploy officers to a new property? | For standard deployments, coverage typically begins within 72 hours of signing. For urgent incidents we have mobilized teams in under 24 hours. Your advisor confirms timing during the initial consultation. |
| Do you provide armed or unarmed officers? | Both. Stratton deploys armed officers, unarmed uniformed officers, plainclothes teams, and concierge-style lobby ambassadors. The posture is matched to the property and the risk profile. |
| What areas do you serve? | Stratton covers Los Angeles County, including Beverly Hills, Santa Monica, Century City, Westwood, Downtown LA, Hollywood, Pasadena, Glendale, Burbank, Malibu, Long Beach, El Segundo, and the South Bay. |
| Do you offer security for one-time events? | Yes. Stratton handles galas, conferences, weddings, private events, and venue-wide functions. We advance the venue, size the team appropriately, and provide a post-event debrief. |
| Do you provide fire watch when sprinklers or alarms are down? | Yes. Trained fire watch officers patrol on the required intervals and maintain the written logs your fire authority expects, until the system is formally restored. Urgent requests are prioritized. |
| How is pricing determined? | Pricing is built around the program, not a flat menu rate — coverage hours, number of posts, armed vs. unarmed, officer experience, and site complexity. You receive an itemized written proposal after the assessment. |
| We're unhappy with our current guard company. Is switching difficult? | No. Transitions are routine and we handle them so coverage is continuous with no gap. We map your existing post orders, identify what's been falling short, and stand up our officers on your timeline. |

**Then set a reminder to check Q&A weekly.** Google does not reliably notify you when someone posts a question. An unanswered question — or worse, a wrong answer from a stranger — sits on your profile indefinitely.

### 1.13 Posts — a weekly cadence for the first 90 days

**Set expectations honestly:** Google Posts are not a ranking lever. They will not move you up the map pack. What they *do* is (a) give someone who found your profile a reason to believe the business is active and staffed, (b) occupy real estate on your branded search result, and (c) create fresh, dated, factual content that AI answer engines and Google's own systems can read. Thirteen posts over 90 days is a reasonable, sustainable investment. Thirty is not, and burning out at week four looks worse than never starting.

**How to post:** From the profile, choose **Add update** (or "Post"). Each post gets: one real photo, 150–300 words of text (the first ~80 characters are what shows before "Read more," so front-load), and a button. Use **Call now** for urgent-need topics and **Learn more** pointing to the exact matching page on strattonsecuritygroup.com for everything else.

# Section 2 — The Google Review Engine

**Prerequisite:** everything in this section requires a claimed and *verified* Google Business Profile. Until verification clears, there is no review link to send. Do not start asking clients until the profile is live — you will burn goodwill you can only spend once.

---

### 2.1 Why Google reviews specifically

Stratton currently has **zero Google reviews**. Competing LA guard companies typically sit somewhere between 20 and 150. This one gap does more damage than any other item on this playbook, for two separate reasons.

**Reason 1 — the local pack.** When someone searches "security guard company near me" or "security company Beverly Hills," Google shows a map with three businesses above the regular results. That box is called the local pack, and it takes the majority of the clicks. Google picks those three on relevance, distance, and *prominence* — and review count and rating are the biggest inputs to prominence that you control. A profile with zero reviews is very hard to rank in the pack even when everything else is right.

**Reason 2 — AI answers.** When someone asks ChatGPT, Gemini, or Perplexity "who's the best security company in Los Angeles," the model doesn't have an opinion. It reads sources and repeats what several of them agree on. Google reviews are the single most-quoted corroboration source for local businesses — they're public, dated, attributed to real names, and mirrored across dozens of aggregator sites. A company with 40 reviews averaging 4.9 gets named. A company with zero has nothing for the model to point at, no matter how good the website is.

Testimonials on strattonsecuritygroup.com do **not** substitute. Anyone can write their own testimonials, so neither Google nor an AI model treats them as independent evidence. Reviews on a platform Stratton doesn't control are the only thing that counts as corroboration.

**Honest framing:** this is slow. Reviews compound over 6–18 months. There is no version of this that works in three weeks, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling fake reviews.

---

### 2.2 The rules — read this before you ask anyone

These are not suggestions. Violating them risks the entire profile being suspended, which is far worse than having no reviews.

| Never do this | Why |
|---|---|
| **Gating** — screening clients first and only sending the link to the happy ones | Explicitly against Google policy. This includes "how did we do?" surveys that route 5-star answers to Google and everyone else to a private form |
| **Incentives** — discounts, gift cards, free hours, raffle entries, "we'll knock $100 off" | Against Google policy *and* illegal under the FTC's rule on fake and incentivized reviews, which carries real civil penalties |
| **Employees or family reviewing Stratton** | Conflict-of-interest violation. Google detects it and it looks obvious to anyone reading |
| **Writing reviews for a client**, even with their blessing | Fake review. Same rule |
| **Buying reviews from any service** | Detected, removed, profile penalized |
| **Asking a client to remove or edit a bad review** as a condition of anything | Review manipulation |

**The one rule that keeps you safe:** ask *everyone* who reaches a natural satisfaction moment, and send the same link and the same message to all of them. You are allowed — encouraged — to time the ask well. You are not allowed to pre-screen who gets asked.

### 2.3 Who asks

The ask has to come from the person the client already knows and trusts. A generic email from "Stratton Security Group" gets ignored; a text from the account manager they spoke to last Tuesday does not.

| Client type | Who asks | Fallback |
|---|---|---|
| Property management / commercial accounts | The account manager or supervisor who runs their site | Omar |
| Event clients | The event lead who was on-site that night | Whoever booked the job |
| Construction / long-term posts | Site supervisor, then account manager follow-up | Account manager |
| Anyone Omar personally sold | Omar, directly | — |

**The office manager's job** is not to send the asks. It's to run the tracking sheet: flag when a moment happens, tell the right person to ask, log the outcome, and send the one follow-up. That split matters — the personal ask converts, the systematic follow-up is what actually produces reviews.

### 2.4 When to ask — the four moments

Ask within **48 hours** of one of these. Satisfaction decays fast; a month later the same client will mean to do it and never get to it.

1. **After the first monthly report is delivered and reviewed.** The client has just seen a month of clean logs, patrol timestamps, and no problems. This is the highest-conversion moment for recurring accounts, and it's the one most likely to be skipped because nothing dramatic happened.
2. **After an incident is handled well.** A trespasser removed, a break-in attempt stopped, a medical call managed, a difficult person de-escalated. Wait until the incident report is delivered and the client has responded. Do not ask in the middle of an active situation.
3. **When an event wraps.** Ask the event lead at load-out while everyone is still standing there, then follow up by text the next morning.
4. **When a client says something unprompted.** "Your guys have been great." "Best company we've used." That sentence is the opening. Respond in the moment: *"That means a lot — would you be willing to put that in a Google review? I'll text you the link."*

**Never ask** during a billing dispute, a staffing gap, an active complaint, or a contract renegotiation.

---

### 2.5 How to ask — the two-step flow

The order is what makes this work. Do not skip step one.

**Step 1 — Ask out loud first.** In person or by phone. A verbal yes creates a small commitment; a cold link does not. It also lets you hear hesitation, which is information you want.

> *"Before I go — would you be open to leaving us a Google review? It genuinely helps people find us."*

**Step 2 — Send the link within the hour, while it's fresh.** Text if you already text that client in the normal course of business; email if that's your channel with them. One message, one link, no attachments.

**Step 3 — One follow-up, 5–7 days later.** Exactly one. Never a second. A person who ignores two nudges will not respond to a third, and you still have to work with them.

**Practical notes that decide whether this converts:**

- The client must be signed into a Google account. Most people on Android or with Gmail already are. On an iPhone with no Google account, they'll hit a login wall and give up — if you're standing next to them, that's the moment to say "no problem, whenever you're at your desk."
- Ask **one named person**, not "the team." Reviews come from individuals.
- Never ask the same person twice for the same account.

### 2.7 Cadence target — steady beats bursty

| Period | Target | What it gets you |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–90 | **10–15 reviews** | Enough to stop looking new. Below ~5, the profile reads as untested |
| Ongoing | **2–4 per month, every month** | Sustained local-pack competitiveness |
| Month 12 | **35–50 total, 4.7+ average** | Genuinely competitive with established LA firms |

At 2–4 per month, roughly 8–10 asks per month against Stratton's active account base is enough. That is entirely achievable — but only if someone owns it. Unowned, it produces zero.

**Why steady beats bursty:** thirty reviews in one week and nothing for eight months is the exact pattern of a company that bought reviews. Google's filters treat sudden clustering as a spam signal and can suppress or remove the batch. A person reading the profile sees the same thing — all reviews dated the same week reads as manufactured. A consistent trickle reads as a real business with real clients, and it tells Google the business is currently active, which a burst does not.

Also: **recency carries weight.** Twelve reviews with the newest from two years ago is a weaker signal than six reviews with the newest from last month. This never becomes "done."

### 2.9 Responding to reviews

**Respond to every review — positive and negative — within 48 hours.** Google states that responding is a factor it considers, and every prospect reads the responses. An unanswered review looks like an absent business; a defensive response loses you clients who were never going to read past it.

Responses are written from the business account under **Reviews** in the Google Business Profile panel.

#### Positive reviews — 4-part structure

1. **Thank them by name.** "Thank you, Marcus."
2. **Name something specific from their review.** Proves a human wrote it. "Glad the overnight patrol coverage has made a difference at the Wilshire property."
3. **Reinforce one true fact naturally** — a service, a location, a standard. Not stuffed. This is what a search engine or AI model actually extracts. "Our licensed officers have been running that site since March."
4. **Close warmly, no sales pitch.** "We appreciate the partnership — the team's glad to be there."

> **Example:**
> "Thank you, Marcus. Really glad the overnight patrol coverage at the Wilshire property has made the difference you were after — our officers have enjoyed working with your on-site team since March. We appreciate you taking the time, and we're here anytime you need us."

**Keep responses short (2–4 sentences) and varied.** Ten identical "Thank you for your feedback!" replies read as automated and undo the benefit.

#### Negative reviews — the discipline

Assume every prospect will read this exchange. You are not writing to the reviewer; you are writing to the next fifty people deciding whether to call.

**Do:** acknowledge, take it offline, stay short, stay calm, sign with a name.
**Do not:** argue facts, mention the contract, or reveal *anything* about the client's site, staffing, or any incident. Confidentiality is a core part of what Stratton sells — a public response that discusses a client's security posture does more damage than the review.

**Structure:**

1. Thank them for raising it — no sarcasm, no "we're sorry you feel that way."
2. Acknowledge the concern without conceding facts you haven't verified.
3. Move it offline with a real name and direct contact.
4. Stop. One response only.

> **Example:**
> "Thank you for raising this, and I'm sorry the service didn't meet the standard we hold ourselves to. I'd like to understand what happened and make it right. Please reach me directly at (424) 440-5554 or Info@StrattonSecurityGroup.com. — Omar, Stratton Security Group"

**Then call them.** Most negative reviews from real clients get revised or removed after a genuine phone call — but only the reviewer can change it, and **you may never make anything conditional on them doing so.** Fix the problem because it's the right thing to do. If they choose to update the review, that's theirs to decide.

#### Reviews that are unfair, mistaken, or fake

This happens in security: a trespasser who was removed, a resident annoyed at being asked for ID, a competitor, or a review that's plainly for a different company.

**A review can be reported only if it breaks Google's content policy** — not because it's harsh or wrong. Reportable grounds:

- **Not a real experience** — the reviewer was never a customer (a competitor, a bot, or a third party who was denied entry at a client's site — they are not a Stratton client)
- **Off-topic** — about something unrelated to the business
- **Conflict of interest** — competitor or former employee
- **Harassment, hate speech, profanity, or personal attacks on a named officer**
- **Personal information** — publishes an officer's full name plus other identifying details
- **Wrong business** — clearly describes a different company

**How to report:**

1. Google Business Profile → **Reviews** → find the review → three-dot menu → **Report review**.
2. Pick the closest policy category. Be accurate — "this is untrue" is not a category and gets auto-declined.
3. Write one or two factual sentences. No emotion. *"This reviewer has never been a client. This account has no service relationship with Stratton Security Group."*
4. Log the date in the tracking sheet.
5. If nothing happens in 5–7 days: search **"Google Business Profile review management tool,"** sign in, and check the appeal status. Declined reports can be appealed once there.

**Be realistic about this:** most removal requests are declined, the process is automated, and it can take weeks. Plan on the review staying up. **Your public response is your real remedy** — it's read by more people than the review itself, and a calm, professional reply next to an unhinged one-star wins more business than the removal would have.

The other real remedy is volume. One unfair 1-star against 8 reviews is a 4.5 average and highly visible. Against 45 reviews it's a 4.8 and nobody scrolls to it. **Burying beats fighting.**

# Section 3 — Citations, Directories & Entity Consistency

A "citation" is just any place on the internet that lists your business name, address and phone number — a directory, a chamber of commerce page, a Facebook profile, an industry association member list. Google and the AI answer engines read those listings to answer two questions: **does this company actually exist, and is it who it says it is?**

Right now Stratton has almost no citations. That is the cheapest gap on this list to close — most of what follows is free, and an office manager can do the whole Tier 1 list in about a working day, spread over a few weeks of waiting for verification.

---

### 3.1 NAP consistency — what it means and why the details matter

**NAP** = **N**ame, **A**ddress, **P**hone. "NAP consistency" means every listing everywhere shows the *same* three things, written the *same* way.

Here is the honest version of why it matters. Google is good at working out that "10940 Wilshire Blvd Ste 1720" and "10940 Wilshire Boulevard, Suite 1720" are the same place — you will not be penalised for a comma. What consistency actually buys you:

- **Verification goes smoothly.** Google, Apple and Bing check your listing against other records (the BSIS licence record, your phone carrier, your business filing). Matching records = fast approval. Mismatched records = rejected verification and weeks of appeals.
- **No duplicate or "ghost" listings.** Two slightly different versions of Stratton can end up as two separate business entities in Google's index, splitting your reviews and your ranking signal in half. Merging them later is a painful support ticket.
- **AI models quote what they read.** When ChatGPT is asked "who's a licensed security company in Century City," it assembles an answer from scraped text. If five sources agree on one phone number and licence number, it repeats them confidently. If sources disagree, it hedges — or skips you and names a competitor it's more sure about. Agreement across sources is what earns a mention.

**Copy this block. Paste it into every listing, every profile, every form. Do not retype it from memory.**

```
Stratton Security Group
10940 Wilshire Blvd, Suite 1720
Los Angeles, CA 90024
(424) 440-5554
Info@StrattonSecurityGroup.com
https://strattonsecuritygroup.com
Hours: Open 24 hours, 7 days a week
California PPO License #122163
```

Save that as a note on the office manager's desktop called `NAP-MASTER.txt`. Every future listing is a copy-paste from that file — never a retype.

**The rules that are genuinely non-negotiable:**

| Rule | Do this | Not this |
|---|---|---|
| Business name = legal/real-world name only | `Stratton Security Group` | `Stratton Security Group - Los Angeles Security Guards` |
| One phone number, everywhere | `(424) 440-5554` | A tracking number on one site, a cell on another |
| Same physical suite everywhere | `Suite 1720` | Omitting the suite on some listings |
| Website always the same root | `https://strattonsecuritygroup.com` | A mix of `www.` / non-`www` / `http://` |

> **Keyword-stuffing the business name is the most common way security companies get their Google listing suspended.** Google explicitly forbids it, competitors report it, and a suspension can cost you weeks. Your name is your name.

### 3.5 BBB accreditation — an honest answer

Two separate things share the BBB name, and they are constantly confused:

- **A BBB listing** — free. Any business can be listed; one for Stratton may already exist unclaimed. Claim it and correct the NAP. **Do this.**
- **BBB Accreditation** — a paid annual membership, typically **$500–$1,500+/year** for a business Stratton's size in the Los Angeles region (they quote based on employee count and won't publish a price). It gets you the "Accredited Business" seal.

**Does it help SEO?** Essentially no. A BBB profile link is worth roughly what any other directory link is worth. Anyone telling you accreditation moves Google rankings is selling something.

**Does it help sales?** For a security firm, a modest yes — with a specific audience. HOA boards, older property owners, and some institutional/municipal procurement processes still weight BBB. It is a trust industry, and buyers are handing over keys and liability.

**Recommendation:** claim the free listing now; **defer accreditation.** Revisit in six months, and only if (a) Google reviews are flowing and the profile is otherwise strong, or (b) someone in a sales conversation actually asks about BBB. That same $500–$1,500 spent on real photography or a chamber membership will do more this year. If accreditation is bought later, buy it for the sales conversation, not the search engine — and be clear-eyed that BBB ratings can be earned without paying, so the money buys the badge, not the grade.

## Part 1 — Third-party lists, PR and proof

### 4.1 Why this matters (the 30-second version)

When someone asks ChatGPT, Perplexity, or Google's AI Overview for "best security companies in Los Angeles," the model does not have an opinion. It reads a handful of pages that already look like they answer that question — "best of" listicles, directory profiles, review sites, and news articles — and summarises what *they* say.

So the goal is not to convince an AI. The goal is to be **named on the pages the AI reads**, and to be described there consistently — same name, same phone, same address, same license number as on strattonsecuritygroup.com.

### 4.2 How the "best of" listicle sites actually work

Most of them are not journalism. They are automated scoring engines. A crawler pulls in public data about every licensed security company in the metro area, scores it, and publishes the top N. Nobody visits Stratton's office. Nobody calls.

What they typically score on:

| Signal they read | Where they get it | Stratton's status today |
|---|---|---|
| Star rating + number of reviews | Google Business Profile, Yelp, BBB, Facebook | **Weakest link — no GBP, zero Google reviews** |
| Business license / bonding | State license registries (BSIS for CA) | Strong — PPO #122163, verifiable |
| Years in operation | Registrations, website, LinkedIn | Fine |
| Website quality + service detail | Crawls the site | Strong — 10 services, 20 area pages, guides |
| Consistency of name/address/phone | Everywhere they can find it | Good on-site; thin off-site |
| Responsiveness | Whether you answer their contact form | Controllable |

**The single most important consequence:** these sites are scoring an empty Google profile. Submitting to Expertise.com or Three Best Rated *before* the Google Business Profile is claimed and has real reviews is close to wasted effort — the algorithm will score Stratton against competitors with 80–300 Google reviews and rank it below them, and that ranking then gets read by the AI models.

> **Do Sections 1 and 2 first. Then do this.** A reasonable trigger: come back to listicle submissions once the profile is verified and has **at least 15–20 genuine Google reviews.** Below that, Stratton loses the comparison.

### 4.4 The scam version — how to spot it

Emails and calls will arrive saying things like *"Congratulations, Stratton Security Group has been selected as a Top 10 Security Company in Los Angeles — secure your placement for $499."*

Refuse anything that matches two or more of these:

- The **only** way onto the list is payment
- The site has no real readership — no news, no staff, no other content, just city × industry lists
- They promise a specific ranking position, or "guaranteed #1"
- They offer to sell "backlinks," "link packages," or "DA 60 placements"
- The list has 300 near-identical city pages generated from a template

These do not help with Google (which discourages paid links), do not help with AI answers (models weight sources that other real sources reference), and in bulk can actively damage the site. **The honest rule: pay for memberships and advertising with a real audience. Never pay for a ranking.**

### 4.5 Local press & trade angles Stratton can credibly pitch

Press mentions are the raw material that separates "a company with a website" from "a company that experts cite." Four angles Stratton can genuinely speak to, with no invention required:

**Angle A — The Palisades rebuild.**
Rebuilding after the fires means thousands of unoccupied lots and half-built homes holding copper, appliances, lumber, tools, and generators, in a neighborhood with reduced foot traffic. Stratton runs construction-site security and patrol and already services Pacific Palisades and Malibu.
*Pitch line:* "What contractors and homeowners rebuilding in the Palisades should know about securing an active construction site — and the three most common mistakes we see."
*Outlets:* Palisadian-Post, Westside Current, Santa Monica Daily Press, The Real Deal LA, Bisnow LA, LA Business Journal.

**Angle B — The event economy.**
SoFi Stadium and the Intuit Dome have made Inglewood a year-round event district, with the 2026 World Cup and 2028 Olympics ahead. Venues have their own security; what nobody covers is the **spillover** — surrounding retail, restaurants, parking structures, hotels, and residential buildings absorbing 70,000 people on a Tuesday.
*Pitch line:* "The businesses around SoFi and the Intuit Dome aren't covered by stadium security. Here's what event-day coverage actually looks like for a hotel or a strip retail center."
*Outlets:* LA Business Journal, Bisnow LA, Connect CRE, hospitality and retail trades.

**Angle C — What property managers should ask a security vendor.**
The strongest B2B angle, because it is genuinely useful and Stratton is credible on it: PPO license verification through BSIS, guard card status, insurance limits and additional-insured endorsements, supervision ratio, post orders, turnover rate, and what happens at 2am when a guard no-shows.
*Pitch line:* "Twelve questions to ask before signing a security contract — and what the answers should be."
*Outlets:* BOMA Greater LA and IREM chapter newsletters, AAGLA's magazine, Commercial Observer, Security Magazine, Campus Safety.

**Angle D — Fire watch.**
Almost nobody explains this well: when a fire alarm or sprinkler system goes down, the fire department or insurer requires a human fire watch, often within hours, with logs. Property managers usually meet this requirement in a panic. Stratton offers it.
*Pitch line:* "Your sprinkler system just failed inspection. What a fire watch is, when you legally need one, and what the logs have to show."

**How to use a journalist-request service.** These are services where reporters post what they need and businesses reply. HARO/Connectively shut down, so the live options are **Qwoted**, **Featured.com**, **SourceBottle**, and **Help a B2B Writer** (all have free tiers). Also worth following: `#JournoRequest` on X and Bluesky.

The routine that works:

1. Sign up for Qwoted and Featured with Omar's name, title, and CA PPO #122163 in the bio.
2. Set the topic filters to: security, physical security, real estate, property management, construction, Los Angeles.
3. **Once a day, 15 minutes.** Skim the requests. Most days there is nothing.
4. When something fits, reply **within an hour** — speed beats polish. Reporters take the first usable quote.
5. Format: 100–150 words, plain English, one specific and concrete point, then the signature block: `Omar [Last Name], [Title], Stratton Security Group, Los Angeles — CA PPO #122163, strattonsecuritygroup.com`.
6. Never pitch a service. Answer the question asked.

**Be honest about the odds:** expect 1–2 placements in the first three months, and the first ones will be small. That's normal, and small ones still get read by crawlers.

### 4.6 Turning the marquee client list into publishable proof

Right now, 18 real, recognisable clients — LAX, Los Angeles World Airports, Hilton, Caruso, Douglas Emmett, Cushman & Wakefield, W Hotels, sbe, Rosewood, Loews, McShane Construction, Mill Creek, Lincoln Property Company, Optimus Properties, Sierra Pacific, Glaser Property Management, The Bicycle Hotel & Casino, Platinum Motorsport — appear only as a wall of logos. A logo is a claim. A **case study is evidence**, and it is the kind of page that answer engines quote from.

**Before you email anyone — check the contract.**
Large corporate and especially government/airport contracts frequently contain a publicity or confidentiality clause: *no use of our name, logo, or the existence of this contract in marketing without prior written consent.* LAX / Los Angeles World Airports is the highest-risk one here. Have whoever holds the signed agreements search each one for the words "publicity," "press," "marketing," "logo," or "confidential" before any outreach. **If a contract prohibits it, do not publish the name — anonymise instead.** This is not optional caution; naming a client in violation of the contract can cost the account.

**The permission request.** Ask the person you actually work with — the property manager or facilities director — not a generic marketing inbox. Ask by phone or in person first, then send this to confirm in writing:

> **Subject:** Quick request — permission to reference [Property] on our website
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> We're building out a case-studies section on our website and we'd like to write up the work we do at [Property] — the scope, how the program is set up, and what it's solved.
>
> Before we write a word, I want your permission and I want you to control what's in it. Three options, whichever you're comfortable with:
>
> 1. **Full:** we name [Company/Property], use your logo, and include a one-or-two sentence quote from you.
> 2. **Name only:** we name the property, no logo, no quote.
> 3. **Anonymous:** we describe it without naming you at all — e.g. "a 400-unit Class-A office tower in Century City."
>
> Whichever you pick, you'll see the full draft and approve it before it goes live, and we'll take it down any time you ask.
>
> Thanks — [Omar]

Log the answer, in writing, with the date. Keep a simple sheet: **Client | Contact | Permission level | Date granted | Contract clause checked (Y/N) | Draft approved (date) | Published URL.**

**How to anonymise well.** A good anonymised case study is *specific about everything except identity*. Keep the property type, the size, the submarket, and the problem. Drop the name, the exact address, and anything uniquely identifying (a distinctive building nickname, an unusual tenant).

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| "Douglas Emmett" | "a 400-unit Class-A office tower in Century City" |
| "A big hotel in Beverly Hills" *(too vague)* | "a 200-room luxury hotel on the Beverly Hills–Westwood border" |
| "Mill Creek" | "a 300-unit Class-A multifamily community in the South Bay" |
| "McShane Construction" | "a $60M ground-up mixed-use project in Downtown LA" |
| "The Bicycle Hotel & Casino" | "a 24/7 gaming and hospitality property in southeast LA County" |

Never write "a major airport" if there is only one plausible answer and the contract bars disclosure — that is naming them with extra steps.

**The case study template** (aim for 500–800 words, one page each):

1. **The property** — type, approximate size, submarket. (Named or anonymised.)
2. **The situation** — what prompted them to bring in security or change vendors. Be concrete: after-hours trespassing, package theft, a failed inspection, tenant complaints, an incident.
3. **What Stratton deployed** — armed/unarmed, posts, hours, patrol frequency, supervision, reporting, technology. Real operational detail is what makes this credible.
4. **How it ran** — onboarding time, post orders, escalation path, who the client talks to.
5. **The result** — the most honest number available. If there are incident logs, use them ("after-hours trespass reports dropped from roughly 6 a month to under 1"). **If there is no measured number, say what changed qualitatively rather than inventing a statistic.** A made-up "62% reduction" is the fastest way to lose credibility with a buyer who asks how it was measured.
6. **Quote** — if permitted.
7. **Close** — the service page it maps to and the phone number.

Start with **three**: one commercial office, one multifamily/residential, one construction or hospitality. That covers the three biggest buyer types and gives the answer engines three distinct pieces of evidence.

**Then reuse each one:** a LinkedIn post, a line in the relevant service page, a data point in a press pitch, and material for a Qwoted answer.

## Part 2 — Measurement

The point of measurement here is to answer one question each month: *is Stratton being found and named more than it was last month?* Everything below fits in about **60 minutes, once a month.**

### 4.8 Google Search Console (already verified)

**What it tells you:** which search terms show Stratton's pages in Google, how often, and how often someone clicks.

**Monthly:**

1. Open **Performance → Search results.** Set the date range to **Last 3 months**, and toggle **Compare → Previous period.**
2. Note the four top-line numbers: Total clicks, Total impressions, Average CTR, Average position.
3. Click the **Queries** tab. Sort by impressions. Write down any *non-brand* query in the top 20 (anything that isn't "stratton security"). Non-brand growth is the real signal — brand searches just mean people already knew the name.
4. Click the **Pages** tab. Which service or city pages are gaining impressions? Those are the ones worth expanding.
5. Once a quarter, check **Indexing → Pages** for anything newly excluded.

**Honest caveat:** Google does **not** break out AI Overview appearances as a separate line — that traffic is folded into normal web search numbers. So Search Console can show impressions rising while clicks stay flat; that pattern often means Stratton is being shown inside an AI answer the user didn't need to click. That's why the manual audit in 4.11 exists.

### 4.9 Bing Webmaster Tools — set this up, it takes five minutes

**Why it matters more than Bing's market share suggests:** ChatGPT's web search retrieves results from **Bing's index**, and Microsoft Copilot runs on it directly. If a page isn't in Bing's index, ChatGPT's search tool has a much harder time finding it. Bing is roughly 4% of US search traffic and a meaningful share of AI retrieval — that second part is the reason to bother.

**Steps:**

1. Go to **bing.com/webmasters** and sign in (a Microsoft account, or sign in with Google).
2. On the "Add your site" screen, choose **Import from Google Search Console.**
3. Click **Import**, authorise the Google account that owns the Search Console property, pick `strattonsecuritygroup.com`, click **Import** again.
4. Done — verification and the sitemap come across automatically. No code changes, no DNS.
5. Come back in a week and check **Site Explorer / URL Inspection** to confirm the pages are indexed. If key pages are missing, use **URL Submission** to push them (Bing allows a generous daily quota).
6. In **Settings**, confirm **IndexNow** is enabled so new pages get pushed to Bing quickly.

Then check it monthly: **Search Performance** (same idea as Search Console — clicks, impressions, queries) and **Indexing** (pages known vs. indexed).

# What this will and won't do

An honest account of what to expect, so nobody is surprised in three months.

### What it will do

- **Make Stratton eligible for the local map pack.** Today, with no Google Business Profile, Stratton *cannot* appear in the map box for "security guard company near me" at any price. After verification, it can. That is a genuine step change, not an incremental one.
- **Give AI models something to point at.** Right now, when ChatGPT or Perplexity is asked for security companies in LA, there is almost nothing outside strattonsecuritygroup.com that corroborates Stratton's existence. A profile, reviews, a LinkedIn page, Yelp, BBB, Bark and a Bing-indexed site change that from "one source" to "many sources that agree."
- **Convert better even before rankings move.** A profile with 25 real photos, ten described services, answered questions and a handful of reviews wins the comparison against a competitor with a blank listing — regardless of who ranks higher.
- **Produce measurable numbers.** Calls from the profile, discovery searches, click-to-call events, form submissions. Within weeks of verification you will have real data where today there is none.
- **Compound.** Reviews, citations, case studies and press mentions all keep working. Nothing here expires.

### What it won't do

- **It won't work in days or weeks.** Verification alone takes one to three weeks. Local pack movement typically takes **three to six months** and is driven mostly by review velocity — how many reviews arrive, how steadily, and how recent the newest one is. AI citations lag further: expect **six months or more** for the broad questions ("best security company in Los Angeles"), and sooner for the narrow ones (fire watch, construction, a specific city).
- **It won't produce a guaranteed position.** Nobody can promise a map-pack ranking or a spot in an AI answer, and anybody who does is either guessing or planning to break Google's rules on your behalf. What can be promised is that the inputs Google and the models actually weigh will be in place.
- **It won't survive being done once.** Reviews stop counting for freshness. Directories quietly change your data. Q&A gets hijacked. Photos go stale. This is a standing 30-minutes-a-week job for the office manager plus the review asks from account managers — not a project with an end date.
- **It won't outrun a review deficit.** Everything in Section 4 — listicles, PR, case studies — is scored against a profile that currently has zero reviews. Doing that work first is close to wasted effort. Sections 1 and 2 gate the rest of the document.
- **It won't compensate for shortcuts.** A single suspension — from a keyword-stuffed business name, a stock photo, a fake location, or one incentivised review — costs weeks of recovery, sometimes permanently loses the reviews accumulated, and shuts off inbound leads while it's down. Every rule in this document that says "never" is there because the downside is severe and the upside was temporary.
- **It won't replace answering the phone.** A 24/7 listing that rings out at 2am converts a lead into a one-star review. The operational promise and the marketing promise have to match.

**The honest summary:** claim the profile, get real photographs, and start asking clients for reviews within 48 hours of the moments in Section 2.4. Do those three things consistently for six months and Stratton will be competitive in the map pack and beginning to appear in AI answers. Do them for two weeks and stop, and nothing here will have mattered.
